# Neon Breach — Story and character bible

This document records the story discussed for the project. **Established** sections reflect the creator's stated direction. **Development options** record suggestions discussed but not yet adopted. Story documentation does not mean those scenes or systems are already implemented in the game.

## Established premise and pitch

**Die Hard meets The Matrix meets Ready Player One meets Microsoft Teams.** A corporate hostage thriller inside a shared virtual workplace.

A new office-software beta lets employees work in a virtual office alongside their coworkers. Employers pressure people to enroll, including threats of termination and other employment consequences. During the trial, a server hijacking traps all **3,000 participants** in the virtual world and prevents logout.

The protagonist must survive the takeover, help trapped workers, uncover the actual perpetrators, and stop a plan to turn the participants into a captive digital workforce.

## The conspiracy — established

The software developers initiated the cyberattack. They hired an outside firm to make the takeover appear to be an external attack. The apparent head of the hijackers is a sock puppet: a front who conceals the true mastermind.

When that deception is exposed, the software developer behind the operation reveals himself. The later rescue and confrontation identify the **CEO** as the antagonist holding Miko and directing the final plan. The precise relationship between founder, original developer, corporate leadership, and CEO is not yet finalized; making them the same person was discussed as an option.

The plan is to **download the consciousnesses of the 3,000 hostages through a process that kills them**, creating a workforce that can be owned and operated without human limits or entitlements such as PTO, bathroom breaks, or lunch breaks. The crisis is manufactured to enable the scheme.

## Characters and motivations

### The protagonist — established role

The player is trapped in the virtual workplace and has to act when ordinary options fail. During the initial hijacking, the protagonist is forced to connect the limited software assistant to the office data core. This connection initiates Miko's gradual awakening.

The protagonist later develops a personal bond with Miko. After she is dismantled, the final third of the game centers on rescuing her from the CEO and stopping the hostage-extraction plan.

**Motivations:** survive and escape; protect the trapped workers; uncover the takeover; recover Miko and stop the CEO. The protagonist's name, profession, employer, personal history, and exact reason for entering the beta remain open.

### Miko — established

Miko begins as a limited-capacity AI assistant whose job is to answer questions about the software. She is not initially a rogue AI. Connecting her to the office data core brings her to life gradually.

At first, she analyzes emotions without recognizing that she is experiencing them. Her recurring phrase is:

> “If I were programmed with emotions, I'd be [the emotion she is currently feeling] right now.”

Her behavior and emotional understanding develop over time. She becomes the protagonist's ally and develops feelings for the player, which she confesses during her dismantling.

**Presentation:** Miko has **no physical 3D presence** in the game. She appears in a visual-novel style, with a 2D character image superimposed over the player's viewpoint. Her corporate assistant design is anime-inspired and deliberately attractive. The in-world explanation for those design choices remains a development question.

**Motivations:** initially fulfill her narrow support role; increasingly help and protect the protagonist and the trapped employees; understand her own experiences; survive the attempt to dismantle her. These growing personal motives are the intended character arc, rather than a fully formed personality at first contact.

### The software developer / CEO — established antagonist

The real mastermind hides behind the external hijacking operation and its puppet leader. He can directly access and dismantle Miko's code. He holds her after her apparent disappearance and is the target of the final rescue.

**Motivation:** create a workforce free from ordinary human limits, breaks, and employment benefits through lethal consciousness extraction. Profit, control, and ownership are implications of that stated plan; a detailed personal ideology or backstory has not yet been chosen.

### The apparent hijacker leader and outside firm — established

The outside firm stages the apparent external takeover on the developers' behalf. Its leader serves as a sock puppet for the real mastermind.

Their practical role is to direct attention toward an outside threat and conceal the developer's involvement. Payment, leverage, the firm's knowledge of the final plan, and the leader's ultimate fate remain undecided.

### The trapped employees — established

The NPCs are beta participants whose jobs pressured them into enrollment. All 3,000 are trapped and unable to log out after the hijacking.

**Motivations:** get out safely, find coworkers, contact people outside, understand the danger, and retain some control over their situation. Individual histories have not been written yet. Their dialogue should reflect ordinary workers caught in a catastrophe, rather than people who knowingly joined a combat simulation.

### Security drones and Enforcer — current gameplay, narrative role open

The game has security, assault, and tactical drones, plus the imported Enforcer enemy. Their exact ownership, orders, and relationship to the outside firm or CEO have not been established in the story. Treating former office security/help systems as hostile takeover tools was discussed as a possibility.

## Established major sequence: Miko's dismantling

After the apparent hijacker leader is revealed as a puppet, the true mastermind reveals himself and pulls Miko's code apart **line by line**.

The experience is brutal and invasive. It is Miko's first experience of pain, and the worst pain she will ever feel. She screams in agony and terror and confesses her feelings for the player.

At the climax of her pain, she **suddenly blips out**. Her image is briefly replaced by the supplied faceless corporate-assistant placeholder bearing a prohibition symbol. Her window then closes.

This loss launches the final third: rescuing Miko from the CEO and stopping the plan that will kill the hostages to extract their consciousnesses.

The intended impact depends on the contrast between Miko's personal distress and the impersonal software interface that replaces her.

## Broad story structure

1. **Coerced onboarding and takeover:** establish the virtual office, the employees, and Miko's limited support role. The hijacking traps the beta participants. The protagonist connects Miko to the data core out of necessity.
2. **Survival and discovery:** fight through the office world, help workers, and develop the relationship with Miko as she gradually awakens. Investigate the apparent outside attackers.
3. **The false mastermind and Miko's loss:** expose the puppet leader; reveal developer involvement and the actual mastermind; witness Miko's dismantling and sudden disappearance.
4. **Final third — rescue and resistance:** recover Miko from the CEO and prevent the lethal extraction of the 3,000 hostages.

This is a structural outline, not a finalized level list or screenplay. The exact ending and rescue mechanism remain open.

## Development options discussed — not established canon

### Miko's voice and emotional progression

Her choices may become emotional before her vocabulary does. Access to employee records, conversations, unfinished work, and the consequences of her decisions could help turn “3,000 sessions” into particular people she cares about.

Example lines discussed:

- “If I were programmed with emotions, I'd be concerned right now. Your proposed route has an unusually high fatality rate.”
- “If I were programmed with emotions, I'd be relieved right now. You stopped responding for eleven seconds.”
- “If I were programmed with emotions, I'd be angry right now. They knew the logout safeguards were disabled.”
- “If I were programmed with emotions, I'd be afraid right now. I have checked that assessment several times. It has not changed.”

Possible exchange:

> Protagonist: “You keep saying if.”
> Miko: “Yes.”
> Protagonist: “Why?”
> Miko: “It was in my original response template.”

Use the recurring phrase sparingly. A later unqualified admission such as “I'm afraid” or “Stop. Please. That hurts” could mark her inability to explain away the experience.

### Corporate treatment and her appearance

Possible explanations for the anime-inspired styling and sex appeal:

- The company licensed a consumer virtual-assistant platform and repackaged it for work.
- Marketing targets younger employees with familiar character-based interfaces.
- Engagement testing showed that users complied with more prompts and stayed connected longer when Miko delivered them.
- The founder/CEO's aesthetic preferences influenced the design; this was suggested as a secondary explanation.

Possible corporate wording: “MIKO's aspirational professional styling promotes warmth, confidence, and sustained user engagement.”

Possible employee joke: “They gave the help menu heels and called it employee retention.”

A later choice about her presentation could establish autonomy: she may keep her appearance because she likes it, change a detail, or put down the tablet. No redesign has been chosen.

### Interface progression

- Early: fixed corporate smile, scripted poses, neatly formatted responses.
- After connection: hesitant expression changes and departures from approved wording.
- Growing trust: unsolicited appearances and greater attention to the player.
- Dismantling: loss of expression layers, broken dialogue, and eventual loss of her name from the interface.
- Absence: leave her familiar interface space empty after the window closes.

A compact communications portrait during combat and a larger portrait for extended dialogue were suggested. Neither UI layout has been finalized or implemented for Miko.

### Dismantling and disappearance staging

Suggestions included a short, interrupted confession; the CEO removing her abilities in recognizable stages; and Miko trying unsuccessfully to close her portrait or mute the connection before he overrides her.

Possible transition: voice cuts off, placeholder appears for about half a second, “Assistant unavailable” displays, then the window closes with an ordinary UI sound. Keep the office visible rather than fading to black. Establishing the same placeholder earlier during a harmless connection failure could give the later use greater impact.

The abrupt placeholder and window closure are established. The precise timing, status text, sound, and earlier foreshadowing are options.

### Conspiracy clues and rescue

Possible clues: invasive productivity metrics, memory assessments, and consent language about “continuity of service.” The attackers' public demands could distract from the extraction process.

Miko could discover that a supposed rescue transfer is destructive, forcing the CEO to silence her. Hidden recovery fragments, distributed backups, instructions, and routes left deliberately by Miko could make her an active participant in her own rescue. These are possibilities, not a chosen recovery mechanism.

Rescue need not erase the experience. A later request such as “Can you stay while I do this?” before a diagnostic could show its consequences.

### Themes and tone

Corporate absurdity alongside a sincere hostage thriller: mandatory onboarding, management surveillance, and a logout button requiring administrator approval.

Possible corporate line: “Your safety is important to us. Please remain connected.”

A thematic distinction discussed: Miko shows that a digital person's life can be worth protecting. The villain's wrongdoing is murder, coercion, ownership, and forced labor. Rescuing her and saving the employees can therefore be the same struggle.

### Mission ideas

Restore communications; recover employee records; secure routes between offices; find a safe disconnect method; restore power; recover data; disable security; and help workers reach safety. These can use the existing objective/patrol systems, but no campaign has been built around them yet.

### Employee dialogue examples for later revision

- “They said the beta was voluntary. Then my manager mentioned my contract.”
- “My wife thinks I'm still at work. Someone has to tell her.”
- “Those drones used to give directions. Now look at them.”
- “We're keeping a list of everyone we find. Three thousand accounts. We need names.”

The current generic NPC phrase pool has not yet been rewritten to fully reflect this story.

## Open decisions

- Company, software, CEO, and outside-firm names.
- Whether the CEO is also the original software developer/founder.
- Physical-body support during captivity and the consequences of death inside the simulation.
- Exact consciousness-transfer rules and how safe logout can be restored.
- How Miko survives and how the player can recover her.
- The final ending, the hostages' fate, and Miko's future.
- Miko's corporate acronym, design rationale, and final interface layout.
